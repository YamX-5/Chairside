# Clinical Content Standards

*Ported from the web build's `src/ingest/prompts.ts` and `src/content/schema.ts`. These are the rules that made the existing content (in `02_content_export.json`) trustworthy. Follow them for any new case/question written by hand, generated by an LLM, or written with Cowork's help — the rules don't change just because the engine did.*

## The core discipline

Write examination items the way a professor who has chaired an exam committee for forty years writes them. That person's items are known for three things: they are unambiguously answerable from the taught material, they punish shallow reading without being tricky, and the distractors are the mistakes real students actually make.

## Rules for every question / decision option

1. **Focused lead-in.** Must pass the cover-the-options test — a competent student should be able to answer it with the options hidden. `"Which of the following is true about X?"` is forbidden. Ask a specific question with a specific answer.

2. **Vignettes above recall.** Anything beyond a pure recall item opens with a short clinical vignette: patient age/sex, presenting complaint, the findings that matter. Only include findings that bear on the reasoning — padding with irrelevant normal values is a different, bad kind of difficulty.

3. **Homogeneous options.** All options answer the same question and belong to the same category — all diagnoses, or all next steps, or all mechanisms. Never mix a drug with a diagnosis with a lab test. Similar length and grammatical form — a longer, more-qualified option is a giveaway.

4. **Distractors from real errors.** Every wrong option is a mistake a prepared student plausibly makes: confusing two adjacent classifications, applying the right rule at the wrong threshold, choosing the intervention one step too early or too late, remembering a superseded guideline. State, for every wrong option, what reasoning leads there and precisely what makes it wrong (the `rationale` / `feedback` field).

5. **Banned constructions.** No "all of the above", no "none of the above", no negative stems ("which is NOT / EXCEPT"), no absolute qualifiers in the key ("always"/"never"), no overlapping/subsuming options, no grammatical cue where only one option agrees with the stem.

6. **Exactly one defensible answer**, correct according to the source material — not a different guideline you happen to know. If the source's position is outdated or contested, write to the source and note the tension in the explanation. The student is being examined on *this* course.

7. **Difficulty is reasoning depth, not obscurity.** A hard item requires more steps of thinking, not a more forgettable fact. Asking for a number buried in a footnote is a cheap trick; requiring the student to combine a staging rule with a modifying factor and then choose the treatment step is the real thing.

8. **Difficulty mix across a set:** roughly 25% easy (recall of a load-bearing fact), 40% moderate (single-step application), 25% hard (multi-step, or requires ruling out a close alternative), 10% brutal (genuinely hard, still fair, still answerable from the material — never a guess).

9. **Source anchoring.** Every generated item carries a `sourceQuote`: a sentence copied verbatim from the source deck that supports the keyed answer, plus the page it came from. If no such sentence exists, the material doesn't support the item — write a different item. Never paraphrase into the quote field.

10. **Language.** English throughout for clinical terms, drug names, doses, numbers — even inside Arabic-aid text.

## Rules specific to a patient case

- The case is a **sequence of decisions**, not a quiz — each decision is a real clinical fork where the reasoning taught determines the right move.
- Order decisions the way the encounter actually unfolds: **assessment before diagnosis, diagnosis before treatment planning.**
- Exactly one option per decision is graded `best`. A genuinely defensible-but-inferior choice is `acceptable`, and its feedback states what it *costs* the patient (delay, discomfort, a relapse risk) — that gradation is what makes a case feel clinical instead of multiple-choice. `poor` options are choices that would actually harm the patient or derail the workup; their feedback names the harm.
- Feedback is second person, at the moment of consequence: what happens to *this patient* because of what you just chose — not a generic explanation.

## The review pass (do this even without an LLM committee)

Whoever/whatever writes an item, a second pass should check for these failure modes, in order of severity:

1. **Factually unsupported** — the keyed answer isn't supported by the cited source, or the quote doesn't actually appear in the source text. Fatal.
2. **Multiple defensible answers** — a second option is arguably also correct under the source's own framing.
3. **No defensible answer** — the key is wrong, or the correct answer is missing from the options.
4. **Cueing** — grammatical agreement, option length, absolute qualifiers, or repeated stem wording point at the key.
5. **Unfocused lead-in** — fails the cover-the-options test.
6. **Worthless distractor** — an option no prepared student would ever choose.
7. **Banned construction.**
8. **Trivial** — tests an incidental detail rather than something worth knowing.

Verdict per item: **keep** (sound as written), **revise** (fixable — supply the corrected item), or **cut** (unsalvageable, or already covered better elsewhere). An exam of twelve excellent items beats twenty mediocre ones — if a reviewer keeps everything, it isn't reviewing.

## The deterministic backstop: source verification

This is the one check that doesn't rely on anyone (human or model) grading themselves honestly, and it's cheap to reimplement in any language:

1. Normalize both the quote and the candidate source page: lowercase, unify curly/straight quotes, strip everything except letters/digits/apostrophes, collapse whitespace.
2. Compute **ordered word overlap**: what fraction of the quote's words appear, in order, somewhere in the page text (a strict substring match fails on PDF-extraction artifacts like hyphenation/ligatures — ordered overlap tolerates that without becoming lax).
3. Check the cited page **and its two immediate neighbors either side** — decks split sentences across slides, so an off-by-one page citation is a nuisance, not a fabrication.
4. **Threshold: 0.75.** Below that, the item is dropped — no exceptions, no "it's close enough."

This is what stands between "a professor-quality item" and "a plausible-sounding invention," and it should survive the engine change unmodified.

## Standards already cited in the existing content (for reference)

- **2018 EFP/AAP classification of periodontal diseases** — staging (I–IV, by worst-site interdental CAL + radiographic bone loss + tooth loss/masticatory dysfunction) and grading (A/B/C, by bone-loss-to-age ratio + risk modifiers like smoking/diabetes).
- **Löe & Silness gingival index (1963).**
- **Resuscitation Council anaphylaxis algorithm (adult)** — recognition (A/B/C problem, not skin signs alone), adult adrenaline dosing (0.5 mg of 1:1000 IM, anterolateral thigh, repeat at 5 minutes if no improvement), positioning (flat + legs raised, never stood upright).
- **ABCDE assessment framework.**

Any new content must cite its own standard with the same specificity — a case that says "give the right dose" without naming the guideline it's quoting is exactly the kind of invented-sounding content this whole discipline exists to prevent.
